# Interview Frameworks (Generic)

Reusable, candidate-agnostic interview frameworks. On the first `interview` run this
file is copied into the user's own `interview_playbook.md`, where they add
company-specific and personal notes over time. Nothing here is tied to one person's
history.

---

## 1. Self-rating questions ("rate yourself 1–10 on X")

**What it actually tests:** not your true skill level, but (a) self-calibration and
honesty, (b) whether you can structure an answer instead of just stating a number,
and (c) it sets the difficulty of the follow-up questions that come next.

**Answer template — works for any skill, not just programming languages:**

```
[a believable number, usually 6–8]
+ [2–3 concrete things you can genuinely do]
+ [1 honest limitation — this line matters most]
+ [optional: how that limitation ties to your current growth direction]
```

**Calibration bands — what each range actually means:**

- **5–6:** solid day-to-day professional competence; works independently on
  standard tasks.
- **7–8:** owns complex problems independently; mentors others; has a systematic
  view of the domain's pitfalls.
- **9–10:** reserve for genuine domain authority — years of near-exclusive
  specialization, with contributions recognised outside your own team or company
  (standards work, maintaining widely-used open-source infrastructure, being the
  person other seniors escalate to). Rarely appropriate early in a career, and
  rarely for a skill that is one of several in your stack rather than your singular
  focus.

**Why overclaiming backfires specifically:** a high number invites harder
follow-up questions. If you cannot defend it under pressure, the damage is not
limited to that one answer — it undermines credibility for everything else you
said. A modest, well-defended number is both safer and more persuasive.

## 2. Answer-structuring principles

- **Answer the question actually asked, not an adjacent one.** Under pressure it is
  easy to pattern-match to a similar question you rehearsed and answer that
  instead. Pause half a beat to identify the literal thing being asked before
  starting.
- **For gap or negative-history questions** (why you left, why the pivot): state
  the fact plainly, once, then move forward. Over-justifying a blameless reason is
  what makes it sound like something to hide. One clean sentence, then pivot to
  what came next.
- **Match your example's punchline to the question's angle.** A great, quantified
  story is still the wrong answer if it proves a different point than what was
  asked ("I complete tickets fast" is not the same claim as "I absorb unfamiliar
  business-domain knowledge fast"). Before telling a story, name in your head
  exactly what point it must prove, and check it against the question.
- **Company-specific answers need real company facts, not generic industry
  language.** "I want more variety" or "I want to work in [trendy industry]" is a
  red flag if it does not match what the company does. Reference their actual
  clients, methodology, or differentiators.
- **For "tell me about a mistake/challenge" questions, keep the subject "I"
  throughout the action, not "we".** A team-level process fix reads as deflected
  ownership even when true. Name the specific root cause plainly ("I assumed
  instead of confirming") and state the lesson explicitly in one sentence ("What I
  took away from that was...").
- **For "how do you prioritize/decide X" questions, lead with your own judgment
  criteria, not "I ask my manager."** State the factors you personally weigh, make
  the routine calls yourself, and escalate only genuine tradeoffs against something
  already externally committed — that is what makes escalating look like judgment
  rather than default deference.
- **"How do you react to X" / "how comfortable are you with X" questions have two
  halves — attitude and process. Answer both.** Lead with one sentence stating the
  attitude directly (receptive, comfortable), then the process. Frame re-explaining
  your reasoning after feedback as a collaborative discussion ("we talked it
  through"), not a defense.
- **Never state a strong conclusion without its premise.** "Ideally I'd spend most
  of my time on discussion, not coding" sounds like a red flag alone, but is a
  strong point with its reason attached. Before saying a strong claim, check
  whether it still makes sense stripped of its premise — if not, say the premise
  first.
- **For "why you over other candidates" — each reason must actually differentiate.**
  "I'm fast / reliable / a quick learner" is genuine but not rare. Demote
  generic-but-true strengths to supporting detail under a more specific,
  harder-to-replicate point, and close by naming the combination of your points as
  the differentiator.
- **A personal-growth claim ("X isn't a challenge for me anymore") and a universal
  claim ("X doesn't matter") sound similar but land very differently.** The first
  is safe; the second invites disagreement and can contradict a position you took
  earlier in the same conversation. Check strong claims against what you have
  already said.
- **Reframe pivots and changes as deliberate tradeoffs, not as settling.** "I
  pivoted from X to Y because Z was the more interesting problem" reads as
  engineering judgment; "I couldn't do X so I did Y instead" reads as giving up —
  same facts, different signal.

## 3. Pre-call checklist

- [ ] Read through this playbook once.
- [ ] Review the company-specific self-introduction out loud, hitting the anchor
      points (not memorized word-for-word).
- [ ] Confirm any facts already sent by email (salary, visa/working rights,
      availability) are fresh in memory, ready to repeat verbatim if asked again.
- [ ] Have 2–3 questions ready to ask them.
